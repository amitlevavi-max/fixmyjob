  **Your $15.48 hourly rate appears to be below Connecticut's minimum wage.** As of January 1, 2026, Connecticut's minimum wage is $16.94. That is $1.46 an hour more than you're earning. It will rise to $17.48 on January 1, 2027. Seven years of experience doesn't change the legal floor, and you should be paid above it.
  
  If you work full time, the gap comes to about $58 a week. Since January that could add up to roughly $2,500 in back pay. That's an estimate, and your actual hours matter.
  
  ## What I'd do
  
  1. **Start keeping records today.** Write down your hours each day, including start and end times and travel between clients if you're not paid for it. Save or photograph your pay stubs, schedules, texts from your employer, and your offer letter or contract. Use your own notes, not just what the employer gives you.
  
  2. **Check your pay stubs for these problems:**
     - **Hourly rate:** Is it below $16.94 now? Was it below the minimum before 2026? The rate was $16.35 in 2025 and $15.69 in 2024.
     - **Overtime:** Connecticut employers must pay one and a half times your regular hourly wage for work over 40 hours in a week. Check this especially if you work for more than one client or agency.
     - **Unpaid time:** Look for travel time, required training, or waiting time that isn't paid.
     - **Meal breaks:** Some domestic workers are entitled to a 30-minute meal period on shifts of 7.5 hours or more. Ask a legal aid group whether this applies to you.
     - **Written notice:** Domestic workers are entitled to written notice of their pay rate, hours, job duties, and paid time off, and how to file a complaint. Check whether you ever got this.
  
  3. **Ask your employer or payroll about it, in writing.** Send a short, polite email or letter asking them to confirm your rate and correct it. This can sometimes fix the problem quickly, and it creates a record.
  
  4. **Get free help before or while you act.** Call Connecticut's Statewide Legal Services hotline or go to ctlawhelp.org. Their materials say if you think your employer isn't following the law, you can file a complaint with the Connecticut Department of Labor.
  
  5. **File a wage claim if it isn't fixed.** You can file a Statement of Claim for Wages with the Wage and Workplace Standards Division of the Connecticut Department of Labor. Don't wait long, because wage claims have strict time limits. One legal source lists the division at 450 Columbus Boulevard, Hartford, (860) 263-6790. It's an older source, so confirm the number on the CT DOL website. You can also file with the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division. Federal claims typically have a two-year limit, or three years for willful violations.
  
  6. **Know that you can recover more than the missing pay.** Under Connecticut law, an attorney can file a private lawsuit to recover twice the amount of wages owed, plus attorney's fees and court costs. Many employment lawyers offer free consultations.
  
  ## Protection from retaliation
  
  You have the right not to be fired as retaliation, or for voicing your rights as a worker. If your hours get cut or you're treated worse after you raise the issue, write down the dates and details and tell the legal aid hotline or the Department of Labor.
  
  ## A few caveats
  
  - I don't know your exact situation. That includes whether you work for an agency, are paid through a state-funded program, or are a live-in worker. Rules differ a little in each case, so a legal aid advisor should confirm. Even so, a pay rate below $16.94 is very likely a violation for most home health aides.
  - You also said you're being *treated* unfairly. If that involves harassment, discrimination, unsafe conditions, or lack of workers' compensation coverage, tell me more. Those go to different agencies, such as the CT Commission on Human Rights and Opportunities.

  ## 1. How your pay compares
  
  The sources disagree a lot, so treat these as rough ranges:
  
  - **ZipRecruiter:** the average annual pay for a Delivery Driver in Des Moines is $37,459 a year. Most salaries fall between $32,700 (25th percentile) and $41,000 (75th percentile), and top earners make $47,820.
  - **Glassdoor:** the typical range is $41,631 (25th percentile) to $58,464 (75th percentile).
  - **Indeed:** Des Moines, IA averages $20.78 per hour. That is roughly $43,000 a year for full-time work.
  
  At $34,500, you're near the bottom of the ZipRecruiter range and below the others. With 4 years of experience, you could reasonably ask for more. Pay varies by employer, vehicle type, and whether you're paid hourly or per route, and these sites don't separate those cases cleanly.
  
  ## 2. Legal issues to check
  
  These are the common problems for drivers:
  
  - **Overtime.** Iowa has no state overtime statute, so overtime comes entirely from the federal Fair Labor Standards Act: one and a half times the regular rate after 40 hours in a workweek for employees who are not exempt. If you're paid a flat salary or per day or route and regularly work more than 40 hours, check whether you're actually exempt. Being salaried doesn't automatically make you exempt. To be exempt, an employee must pass both a salary basis test and a duties test.
  - **Misclassification.** If you're paid on a 1099 as an "independent contractor" but the company sets your schedule, routes, and rules, you may be misclassified. Contractors also pay for fuel, insurance, and wear on the vehicle. Those costs can push your real pay well below your headline number.
  - **Unpaid time.** Look for unpaid loading, waiting, or pre- and post-trip time, and for deductions from your pay.
  - **Vehicle costs.** If you use your own vehicle, check whether you're reimbursed for mileage and fuel. If you aren't, your effective hourly rate may be low.
  
  ## 3. What to do
  
  1. **Document everything.** Keep pay stubs, a log of your actual start and end times, routes, and any texts or emails about pay or schedule. Supporting documents like pay stubs strengthen a claim.
  2. **Work out your real hourly rate.** Divide your annual pay by the total hours you actually work, including unpaid time. Subtract any costs you cover yourself.
  3. **Ask your employer.** Request a raise with the market data above and your 4 years of experience, or ask for clarification of how your pay is calculated. Do this in writing if you can.
  4. **File a wage claim if you're owed money.** The Division of Labor at Iowa Workforce Development accepts and investigates wage claims at no cost to the worker. The Division will not accept claims if the money you are owed is more than $6,500.00. For larger amounts, you could go to court or use the federal route. The Department of Labor Wage and Hour Division handles the federal minimum wage and overtime claim.
  5. **Watch the deadline.** Iowa Code 614.1 gives two years for an action founded on a claim for wages. Federal overtime claims can reach three years if the violation was willful.
  6. **Consider an employment attorney.** For unpaid overtime, employers can be held liable for up to twice the amount of unpaid back wages, plus court costs and attorney's fees. Many wage-and-hour attorneys offer free consultations and work on contingency.
  7. **Look at other jobs.** Some listings show Des Moines delivery or local driver roles at $20 - $25 an hour. Applying elsewhere gives you leverage and a fallback.
  
  ## 4. About "treated fairly"
  
  If your concern is about treatment rather than pay, such as discrimination, harassment, retaliation, or unsafe conditions, there are different agencies. Those include the Iowa Civil Rights Commission, the EEOC, and OSHA. Retaliation for raising wage complaints is generally illegal.

  - About how many hours a week do you actually work?
  - Do you use your own vehicle, and are your expenses covered?
  - What specifically feels unfair: the pay, unpaid time, scheduling, how you're treated, or something else?
